      Lesson 04

      Authentication & Authorization

      Two questions, and the entire topic hangs off them. Authentication answers “who are you?” — assigning an identity to a subject in a given context. Authorization answers “what can you do?” — the permissions and capabilities that identity carries. Everything below is machinery for answering one or the other.

      The two-sentence summary

      Authentication is the process of finding the answer to “who are you in this context?” — where the context might be a platform, an operating system, or your phone. Authorization is the process of finding the answer to “what can you do in that context?”

      How we got here

      Authentication has a long history, and knowing it makes the modern designs feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

      • Pre-industrial Implicit trust

        Authentication was intrinsic — identity was the same thing as recognition. A village elder, already respected in the community, could vouch for a person; deals were sealed with a handshake symbolising mutual recognition. Elementary, but it was a real form of auth, based on human contextual trust.

      • As populations grew Implicit trust fails to scale

        Interactions extended beyond familiar circles. The respected elder is not respected in the next village, let alone the next continent. This marked the beginning of the search for explicit authentication — proof of identity that works without personal acquaintance.

      • Medieval Wax seals — the first authentication tokens

        A seal bearing a unique pattern, pressed onto documents and agreements. These were the first widely adopted authentication tokens: physical representations of identity relying on something you have. They were also forgeable — arguably the first recorded authentication bypass attacks — which pushed people towards watermarks, encrypted codes in trade documents, and the beginnings of cryptographic thinking.

      • Industrial revolution Passphrases and shared secrets

        As machines advanced, so did communication. The telegraph became critical infrastructure, and with it came the need to validate messages. Operators used pre-agreed passphrases — an early form of shared secret, and effectively static passwords. The principle had shifted from something you have to something you know.

      • 1961 CTSS, and the printed password file

        Researchers at MIT's Project MAC, working on the Compatible Time-Sharing System, introduced passwords for multi-user systems — letting several users share one computer without sharing each other's data. They stored those passwords in plain text. The vulnerability became undeniable when someone printed the password file. That single incident is the genesis of secure password storage.

      • After CTSS Hashing

        The philosophy that passwords must never be stored in plain text produced hashing: an algorithm that takes plaintext and returns a fixed-length, irreversible representation. Whether you feed it 3 characters or 100, the output length is constant for a given algorithm, and the same input always produces the same output. Authentication began to align with the core tenets of information security — confidentiality, integrity, availability.

      • 1970s Asymmetric cryptography

        An explosion of cryptographic research, driven by people like Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman. The Diffie–Hellman key exchange introduced asymmetric cryptography: two parties establishing a shared secret over an untrusted medium. This became the backbone of every modern authentication protocol, and of public key infrastructure (PKI). The same era produced Kerberos, which introduced ticket-based authentication — a trusted third party issuing tickets that verify both user and service. A direct precursor to token-based auth.

      • 1990s Multi-factor authentication

        As the internet grew, plain username and password proved too weak against brute-force and dictionary attacks. MFA combined the principles instead of choosing between them: something you know (password, PIN), something you have (smart card, OTP generator), something you are (fingerprint, retina). Biometrics arrived as a breakthrough — pattern recognition on unique physical traits — though it brought its own problems: false positives, false negatives, and template security. Not a one-step solution either.

      • 21st century Cloud, mobile, APIs

        Cloud computing, mobile devices and API-based architectures demanded frameworks none of the above could provide. Out of that came the components we actually work with: OAuth and OAuth 2.0, JWTs, zero-trust architecture, and passwordless systems such as WebAuthn, which eliminate passwords entirely in favour of key pairs held in hardware.

      • Next Candidates for the future

        Decentralised identity built on blockchain-style technology — early, experimental, promising. Behavioural biometrics. And post-quantum cryptography: the recognition that once quantum computers are in common use they will break the algorithms — RSA and friends — whose difficulty for today's hardware is the whole reason we feel safe. Post-quantum cryptography is the search for algorithms that stay secure against them. Some already exist.

      Key takeaways

      • The arc runs: who vouches for you → what you hold → what you know → what you are → what you can prove cryptographically.
      • Every step was forced by the previous one failing to scale or being broken.
      • Passwords are never stored in plain text. One printed file settled that in 1961.

      Three primitives you need first

      Sessions, JWTs and cookies come up constantly in everything that follows, so we introduce them up front.

      Sessions

      HTTP was designed stateless (see Lesson 01): every request is an isolated interaction carrying everything the server needs, with no memory of past exchanges. That was ideal for the early web — static pages, static images, content you read and moved on from. Nobody needed continuity between requests.

      Then the web became dynamic, and statelessness turned into a bottleneck. How does an e-commerce site remember what is in your cart? How does a user stay logged in while navigating between pages? Those questions are the beginning of stateful interactions on the web, and sessions are the answer: a temporary, server-side context for each user.

      1. On login The server creates a session

        It generates a unique session ID and stores it alongside the relevant user data — role, name, cart items, whether they are authenticated — in a persistent store. That store might be a database (file-based) or Redis (in-memory).

      2. Back to the client The session ID is sent as a cookie

        The unique key travels to the browser as a cookie. Every subsequent request automatically includes it.

      3. On each request The server looks the session up

        It takes the session ID from the cookie and fetches the user's data from the store. The server now effectively has memory.

      4. Eventually The session expires

        Sessions are short-lived. If the expiry is 15 minutes, then after 15 minutes the presented session ID is dead and the server creates a fresh one. Round and round.

      How session storage evolved

      GenerationWhere sessions livedWhy it changed
      File-based Files on the server. Simple, but did not scale as user counts grew.
      Database-backed A database table. Faster lookups, and sessions survived server restarts.
      Distributed Redis or Memcached — in-memory stores. In-memory means RAM rather than disk, so lookups are considerably faster than hitting a traditional database.

      We still use sessions today, for exactly the original purpose: giving the server memory.

      JWTs

      By the mid-2000s web applications had become globally distributed systems, and stateful sessions — effective as they are — became a bottleneck for two reasons:

      • Memory. Retaining session data for millions or billions of users is a large, costly overhead.
      • Replication. In a distributed architecture, one server may sit in one part of the world and another on the opposite side. Synchronising session data across regions introduces latency — delay in authenticating the user — and consistency problems.

      Developers wanted to offload state from the server without losing security or integrity. JWTs, formalised in 2015, are a stateless mechanism for transferring claims between two parties. The key innovation is that the token is self-contained: it carries the user data and its own cryptographic signature, Base64-encoded.

      Part 01

      Header

      Metadata about the token itself — principally the signing algorithm, so the verifying server knows how the token was signed.

      Part 02

      Payload

      The claims — the data you actually need. Conventional fields:

      • sub — the subject: the user's ID. Which ID is up to you: from your database, from an auth provider, from any context. It is just a storage slot.
      • iat — issued at: when the token was created.
      • Optional fields — name, role (admin, member, editor, viewer), anything else you need.
      Part 03

      Signature

      Proof that you issued this token and that it has not been tampered with. Produced with a secret key only you hold. Change any byte of the payload and verification against your secret fails — so you know immediately it is no longer a valid token.

      Try it yourself

      jwt.io decodes a token in the browser and shows all three parts side by side. Worth pasting one in once so the structure stops being abstract.

      What JWTs bought us

      Advantage 01

      Statelessness

      No server-side storage cost. Verification is a signature check, not a lookup — lightweight, and it happens on every request.

      Advantage 02

      Scalability

      A cookie issued by one server is not much use to a server on another domain — a real problem in microservice architectures. With JWTs, every service holds the shared secret and can verify the token independently. Scale to any number of instances and none of them has to worry about where the session lives.

      Advantage 03

      Portability

      Lightweight, URL-friendly, Base64-encoded strings. They pass between systems easily, fit in cookies, headers and URL values, and work in constrained storage. (They can also be put in localStorage — though you should not.)

      And the two problems they introduced

      Problem 1 — theft

      Because a JWT is stateless there is no stored record the server consults to validate you. If someone obtains your token they can impersonate you and act on your behalf. And there is no way to invalidate that token before it expires — unless the server changes its secret. Which invalidates every token, forcing every user on the platform to log in again, all to revoke one.

      Problem 2 — revocation

      If you need to revoke a specific user's access — compromised account, malicious activity — you cannot. Stateless tokens give you no way to track a token's status; you simply wait for it to expire.

      The hybrid approach, and the honest objection to it

      The usual answer is to combine statelessness with a little statefulness. Normally, a JWT flow does no storage lookup: you verify the signature with your secret, read the user ID out of the payload, done. In the hybrid approach you add one step — after verification succeeds, check the token against a blacklist in your persistent store (a database or Redis). That gives you the ability to temporarily block a user or revoke a compromised session.

      The obvious question

      If the whole point of a JWT was statelessness, and you are now doing a persistent-storage lookup on every request anyway — why not use the stateful approach in the first place, which gives you this for free and is generally considered more secure?

      It is a fair objection, and it comes up constantly in the industry. There is no tidy answer; it is a trade-off you make with your eyes open.

      Practical advice

      For a medium-to-large system, do not agonise over these choices — use an auth provider. Auth0, Clerk and others exist precisely so that the algorithm choice, the hashing, the salting, the token strategy and every other part of building a secure auth system is their problem.

      That said: while you are learning backend engineering, implement your own auth. Understand how the parts fit, what the trade-offs are, where the advantages and disadvantages lie. Then in production, reach for a provider — unless you are genuinely confident in your own workflows.

      Cookies

      A cookie is a way of storing a piece of information — any string, any value — in the user's browser, from the server side. That last part is what makes it useful: through the ordinary HTTP request–response flow, a server can put data in a client's browser.

      Browsers enforce a nice security property here: a cookie set by one server is only accessible to that server. One server cannot read another's cookies. And a cookie set by a server is automatically included in every subsequent request to that server.

      1. Client Authenticates with username and password
      2. Server On success, sets a cookie

        Containing an authorization token — a JWT, a session ID, whatever the implementation uses.

      3. Browser Attaches it to every later request, automatically

        Chrome, Firefox, Safari — all of them. No client code required.

      4. Server Validates the token and identifies the user
      Why cookies matter

      Cookies automate the round trip — server sends the token to the client, client returns it on every request — entirely from the server side. That is the part to hold on to.

      Key takeaways

      • Sessions give a stateless protocol memory, at the cost of a store to keep it in.
      • JWTs move that state into a signed, self-contained token — buying scale, losing revocation.
      • Cookies are the transport that makes either one automatic in a browser.
      • Set HttpOnly and Secure so JavaScript cannot read the cookie and it only travels over HTTPS.

      The four types of authentication

      These are the ones you will meet as a backend engineer in day-to-day work.

      1. Stateful authentication

      1. Client → Server Send credentials

        Username and password, or email and password — whatever the mechanism is.

      2. Server Validate, then create a session

        If the credentials are correct and the user is eligible, the server generates a session ID, bundles it with the user's data, and stores the pair — usually in Redis, because its read times beat a traditional database.

      3. Server → Client Return the session ID in an HttpOnly cookie

        HttpOnly means JavaScript cannot read the session ID at all.

      4. Every request after The cookie rides along automatically

        The server takes the session ID, checks that it exists in Redis, checks its expiry, reads the user data, and identifies and authorizes the user. The API call proceeds.

      The session ID itself can be any cryptographically random string — or even a JWT. That is an implementation detail.

      Pros

      • Centralised control over every session.
      • Real-time visibility — you know which sessions are active right now, because you have a store to ask.
      • Easy revocation — revoke a session, log a user out, at will.
      • Well suited to applications with moderate traffic and strict session requirements.

      Cons

      • Limited scalability — the store is a shared dependency.
      • Higher operational complexity in distributed systems.
      • Latency when servers span multiple regions.
      An opinion worth hearing

      Most applications should probably go with stateful authentication — for the security properties and for the sheer convenience of being able to revoke a token and see who is logged in right now.

      2. Stateless authentication

      1. Client → Server Send credentials
      2. Server Validate, then sign a JWT

        The server holds its own secret key, used to both sign and verify tokens. It must be stored securely. The new token carries the user's ID, role, and whatever else is needed.

      3. Server → Client Return the JWT
      4. Every request after Client sends it in the Authorization header

        You can send it any way you like, but the standard is Authorization: Bearer <token>.

      5. Server Verify the signature and read the identity

        No storage lookup. If verification succeeds the server knows the user's ID and the request proceeds; otherwise it returns 401 Unauthorized or 403 Forbidden.

      Why it earns the name

      In stateful auth you must consult a persistent store. In stateless auth everything you need is inside the signed token, and the secret key is what proves it authentic. A self-contained, self-sustaining credential.

      Pros

      • Scales freely — no session store dependency.
      • Ideal for distributed architectures.
      • Mobile-friendly, where cookies are awkward or absent.

      Cons

      • Revocation is genuinely hard — once issued, a token lives until it expires.
      • A compromised account cannot be cut off on request without rotating the secret and logging everyone out.
      • No real-time view of who is authenticated.
      A pragmatic hybrid

      Split by client type. Your main web app, running in a browser, uses stateful auth — you get revocation and visibility where it matters most. Your mobile apps and third-party server integrations use stateless auth — you get scale and simplicity where cookies are a nuisance. Each side's weakness is covered by using it only where it is a strength.

      3. API key authentication

      This serves a completely different set of use cases from the two above. You visit a platform, click “generate an API key”, and receive a cryptographically random string. That string grants you direct, programmatic access to the server behind the UI.

      The example everyone recognises

      You type into ChatGPT's search bar and get a response. Somewhere in the cloud, many servers produce that response and the client renders it nicely for you to read. That is the UI path.

      But OpenAI also offers API keys — because plenty of people do not want the UI. They want to build their own interface for their own use case, or they want the model's response inside their own server, programmatically. API keys give access to the server, not the platform — in a confined, permission-scoped, expiry-bound way.

      Client-to-server vs machine-to-machine

      Interaction A

      Client to server

      What we normally build. A human interacts visually — mouse, keyboard, a UI. The UI sends programmatic requests, the server responds, the UI renders the result for the human to read and act on. There is a human trigger at the start of it.

      Interaction B

      Machine to machine

      My server has a UI, and a user types a paragraph and asks it to summarise. My server wants to use ChatGPT's capabilities to do that. So my server calls their server, using an API key, gets the summary back, and returns it to my UI. No UI on that hop, and no human interaction anywhere in it.

      This is precisely why API keys fit machine-to-machine work. A stateful or stateless login flow needs a human trigger: a form to type into, a submission, a token to store. A machine has none of that. Instead you give it one secret, it saves it in an environment variable or a secret store, and it sends that on every request to identify itself. Much simpler for the shape of the problem.

      Good for

      • Trivial to generate — click a button and use it.
      • Server-to-server and machine-to-machine communication.
      • Single-purpose programmatic access to an API.
      • Per-key permissions, quotas and expiry, tracked server-side.

      Watch out for

      • A long-lived secret in plain form — leak it and it is fully usable.
      • No user identity; the key is the identity.
      • Must be stored in environment variables or a secret manager, never in client code.

      4. OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect

      Everything so far has been a simple setup: create an account with a username and password, prove it, get a token. The more platforms you use, the more credentials you have to create, remember and maintain. Two problems followed:

      • Security risk. Reusing passwords was extremely common — 12345, password — so one breach compromised many accounts.
      • Fatigue. The sheer number of accounts to manage was overwhelming.

      The delegation problem

      Then a new class of problem appeared: one platform needing access to another platform's resources.

      • A travel or hotel-booking app that wants to scan your Gmail for flight tickets, programmatically.
      • A social media app that wants to import your contacts from another platform, or from Google Contacts.

      That is delegation: a user granting one platform programmatic access to their resources on another platform.

      The solution users actually reached for — and why it was a disaster

      They shared their passwords. Which meant:

      • Full access to everything. No way to limit permissions to just the thing you wanted to share.
      • No way to revoke. Taking access back meant changing your password — and since people reused passwords, changing it everywhere.

      2007: OAuth — open authorization

      Engineers from companies including Google and Twitter began standardising a way for users to share access from one platform to another without sharing passwords. Their answer: share tokens instead.

      Password vs token

      Give me your Google password and I can do anything: read your Photos, browse your Maps history, see your Contacts, add to and delete from your Calendar. Everything.

      Give me a token scoped to reading your contacts and I can read your contacts. Not delete them. Not add to them. Nothing else, anywhere. Specific permission, for a specific part, of a specific platform.

      The four roles

      Using the example of a social media app wanting your Google contacts:

      RoleWho it isIn this example
      Resource owner The user who owns the data. You.
      Client The app requesting access. The social media app.
      Resource server Where the data lives. Google's servers, holding your contacts.
      Authorization server Who authenticates the user and issues the token. Google's authorization server.

      The OAuth 1.0 flow

      1. Client Redirects the user to the authorization server

        The social media app sends you to Google.

      2. User Authenticates and grants permission

        You log in on Google, and consent to the specific permissions being requested.

      3. Authorization server Sends a token to the client

        Google issues the token to the social media app.

      4. Client Uses the token against the resource server

        The app reads your contacts from Google — programmatically, without ever seeing your password, and only within the permissions you granted.

      2010: OAuth 2.0

      OAuth 1.0 was revolutionary but had two practical limitations: the flow was complicated for developers to implement, and it relied on cryptographic signatures, which were error-prone. OAuth 2.0 addressed both:

      • It introduced bearer tokens — far simpler across the whole flow. More vulnerable in principle, but dramatically easier to implement correctly.
      • It let developers choose a flow based on the app type, rather than forcing one shape on everything.
      FlowBuilt for
      Authorization code Server-side apps.
      Implicit Browser-based apps. Now discouraged, due to security risks.
      Client credentials Machine-to-machine — two servers talking, no user, no browser, no human.
      Device code Devices with limited input — no keyboard or mouse. Smart TV sign-in uses this.

      2014: OpenID Connect fills the gap

      What OAuth did not solve

      OAuth was excellent at authorization — what you are permitted to do. It did not solve authentication — who you actually are. Delegation was handled; identity was not.

      OpenID Connect (OIDC) was built on top of OAuth 2.0's security mechanisms to close that gap. Its contribution is the ID token — which is, in practice, a JWT. It carries:

      • The user's ID.
      • When they logged in (via iat).
      • The issuing authority — which platform issued this token.
      • The user's name, email, and whatever profile information the requesting party asked for.

      This is what “Sign in with Google”, “Sign in with Facebook” and “Sign in with Discord” run on. The platform takes your identity — email, name, profile picture — from Google, and either stores it or simply uses it to authenticate you, without ever building its own authentication mechanism.

      The OIDC flow, end to end

      Say you open a note-taking app and click “Sign in with Google”.

      1. Client Redirects you to the authorization server

        The note-taking app sends you to Google.

      2. User Logs in on Google and grants the requested permissions

        You authenticate against Google's authorization server, not the app's, and consent to what it asked for — your email, your name, your profile picture, perhaps access to your Google Keep notes.

      3. Authorization server Returns an authorization code (and optionally an ID token)

        Back to the note-taking app.

      4. Client Exchanges the code for an access token

        The app trades the authorization code for an access token — and an ID token, if it did not already get one. The ID token is the JWT holding your identity: your ID, your name, your details.

      5. Client Acts on your behalf, within scope

        With the access token stored, the note-taking server can now fetch all your Google Keep notes from Google's servers on your behalf. It can appear as you to Google — but only for the things you granted. Nothing else.

      The right way to picture OAuth 2.0 and OIDC

      Think of them as the security guards, or the key-makers, of the digital age. They ensure that no one — not a user, not a platform — gets more access than they need or than they asked the user for in the first place. Together they turned the internet from password-sharing chaos into a secure, interconnected system, and they are the reason we can integrate one system into another at all.

      Which one should you use?

      TypeUse it forBecause
      Stateful Web app authentication — which is most SaaS products. User-specific session data lives on the server, so you keep control, visibility and revocation.
      Stateless APIs and scalable systems with servers distributed worldwide. The token carries the user's data, so no server needs a shared store.
      OAuth / OIDC Third-party integrations, and login via external providers. Delegated, scoped access without password sharing — plus identity, from OIDC.
      API keys Server-to-server and machine-to-machine, or single-purpose API access. No human in the loop, so no login flow is needed — just one scoped secret.
      In practice

      When you are building APIs, you will spend most of your time in stateful and stateless authentication. The other two are for specific situations you will recognise when you meet them.

      Key takeaways

      • Stateful = server remembers, you can revoke. Stateless = token remembers, you cannot.
      • API keys exist because machines have no hands to type a password with.
      • OAuth solved delegation by replacing shared passwords with scoped tokens.
      • OIDC added the missing identity layer on top, as an ID token that is a JWT.
      • A hybrid split — stateful for browsers, stateless for mobile and integrations — is often the right answer.

      Authorization

      Not as vast a topic as authentication, but you need the shape of it. Recall the one-line distinction: authentication is who, authorization is what you may do.

      Why it had to be invented

      Take a note-taking platform. A user logs in with valid credentials and can now create, update and delete notes. Fine.

      Now add a feature: deleting a note does not actually destroy it — it goes to a recycle bin. The user can delete it permanently, or after 30 days it moves somewhere else automatically: deleted for the user, but not gone from the database. Call that the dead zone.

      As the creator of the platform, you want to inspect the dead zone programmatically through a separate admin UI. So you need capabilities that ordinary users must not have.

      The tempting bad solution

      Invent a random string — a god-mode string — and send it with each admin API call. The server recognises it and grants the extra capabilities.

      Two things go wrong.

      • Security. That string is travelling in your API calls. Anyone who intercepts it can wipe the database, tamper with other users' data — anything.
      • It does not extend. Want to give the same access to a friend who helps maintain the platform? Either hand them the same string, or mint more strings and teach the server about each one. The system is now more complicated, harder to manage and more exposed than before.
      Which is to say

      Granting specific permissions to specific users is authorization. Not every user has the same level of access — some have more capabilities, some fewer, some an entirely different set. And if you are building multi-tenant software, where users create organisations and admins assign read or write permission to individual members, you need this to be a first-class part of the design rather than a pile of magic strings.

      RBAC — role-based access control

      The most widely used answer, and the one to be familiar with. It works exactly as the name says: the platform defines roles, and each role is assigned a set of permissions over particular resources.

      RoleNotes resourceDead zone resource
      user read, write, delete (own notes) — no access
      moderator read, write read
      admin read, write, delete read, purge

      You can define your own custom roles with their own permission sets, and go as granular as you need — per role, per permission, per resource.

      How it runs in a request

      1. Sign-up The server assigns a role

        A user role by default, or something else depending on the use case.

      2. Each request The client sends its credential

        A session ID for stateful auth, a JWT for stateless auth — whatever the mechanism.

      3. Entry point The server deduces the role

        Either straight out of the token, or with a database lookup. It then attaches that information to the request so the code that runs next can see it. (We cover middleware chains in a later lesson; for now, picture the first thing the request touches establishing identity and role.)

      4. Downstream Later logic decides

        Can this request read the dead-zone notes? Yes if the role is admin. If the role is user, the API fails and the server returns 403 Forbidden — you do not have permission to perform this action or access this resource.

      Key takeaways

      • Authorization exists because not all authenticated users are equal.
      • RBAC: roles → permissions → resources. Custom roles as granular as you like.
      • Resolve the role early in the request, attach it, and let downstream logic branch on it.
      • The refusal is 403, not 401 — you know who they are.

      Two security details that catch people out

      Since this is authentication in a backend context, two things are worth knowing before you write any of it.

      1. Never send helpful error messages

      During authentication you will be tempted to be helpful. The user logs in with an email you cannot find, so you return “user not found”. The email exists but the password is wrong, so you return “incorrect password”. They fail a few times, so you return “account locked due to too many failed attempts”.

      These messages are helpful to legitimate users. They are also clues for attackers:

      Leak 01

      “User not found”

      The attacker now knows this username does not exist. They cross it off and move to the next one on their list — you have just helped them enumerate your users.

      Leak 02

      “Incorrect password”

      The attacker now knows the username is correct. Only the password remains, so they point brute-force or dictionary attacks at it. You have narrowed their attack surface for them.

      The rule

      Keep every authentication message generic. One string — Authentication failed — for all cases, regardless of what actually went wrong. The attacker is left with no idea what to try next.

      This applies specifically to the authentication lifecycle. Elsewhere — validation errors, ordinary API responses — be as user-friendly as you like.

      2. Timing attacks

      Rarer, but real. Consider what a login handler actually does, in order:

      1. Step 1 Find the user

        Look up the provided email or username. Does this user exist?

      2. Step 2 Check whether the account is locked

        Many servers lock an account for two minutes, four minutes, a day, after too many failed attempts.

      3. Step 3 Compare the password against the stored hash

        Passwords are never stored in plain text. On sign-up the password is hashed into a cryptographically safe string and only the hash is stored — there is no way to recover the plaintext, not even for the server. So to check a login, the server hashes the submitted password with the same algorithm, fetches the stored hash, and compares the two.

      The leak

      If the username is invalid, the handler terminates at step 1 — fast. If the username is valid but the password is wrong, it runs all the way through step 3, and hashing is comparatively expensive, so the response is measurably slower.

      An attacker who measures that difference — 200 ms, whatever it is — learns which of the two failed, even though your error message said nothing. They now know whether to attack the username or the password, and can strategise accordingly.

      Two defences

      Defence 01

      Constant-time comparison

      Use the cryptographically secure constant-time comparison functions your platform provides for password hashes. Their execution time does not vary with how similar the inputs are, so there is nothing to measure.

      Defence 02

      Equalise the response time

      Simulate the missing work. In Node that is a setTimeout; in Go a time.Sleep. Even when the username does not match, do not answer instantly — add a deliberate delay so the fast path and the slow path look identical from outside.

      The principle behind both

      An authentication system must equalise its response times. Anything an attacker can measure is something you are telling them.

      Key takeaways

      • One generic failure message for the entire auth lifecycle. No exceptions.
      • Timing is a side channel — a fast rejection reveals which check failed.
      • Use constant-time comparison, and pad the fast path so both paths take the same time.

      Where that leaves us

      Authentication answers who are you; authorization answers what can you do. Between them sit sessions, JWTs and cookies as primitives; stateful, stateless, API-key and OAuth/OIDC as strategies; RBAC as the standard way to express permissions; and two quiet security details — generic errors and equal response times — that separate a working implementation from a safe one.

      Key takeaways

      • Authentication establishes identity; authorization checks permission for an operation.
      • Sessions, JWTs and cookies are primitives; choose a strategy that fits your trust and revocation needs.
      • Enforce permissions on the server and avoid leaking account information through failures.
